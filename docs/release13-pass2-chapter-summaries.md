## Chapter 3. Definitions from the Mental Health Act IRR

The source states that the following terms are defined by the Mental Health Act Implementing Rules and Regulations. The summaries below preserve the substance of each definition.

1. **Mental Health.** A state of well-being in which a person realizes their abilities and potentials, copes adequately with ordinary life stresses, shows resilience in extreme life events, works productively and fruitfully, and can contribute positively to the community.
2. **Mental Health Professional.** A medical doctor, psychologist, nurse, social worker, guidance counsellor, or another appropriately trained and qualified person with specific skills relevant to mental health services.
3. **Mental Health Worker.** A trained person, volunteer or advocate involved in mental health promotion and support services under a mental health professional’s supervision.
4. **Mental Health Service Provider.** A public or private entity or individual providing services as defined in the Mental Health Act. The source’s examples include mental health professionals and workers, social workers and counsellors, peer counsellors, informal community caregivers, advocates and their organizations, personal ombudsmen, and persons or entities offering non-medical alternative therapies.
5. **Mental Health Services.** Psychosocial, psychiatric or neurologic activities and programs across the support continuum, including promotion, prevention, treatment and aftercare, provided by mental health facilities and mental health professionals.
6. **Service user.** A person with lived experience of a mental health condition, including a person who requires or is receiving psychiatric, neurologic or psychosocial care.
7. **Carer.** A person who may or may not be the patient’s next of kin or relative, maintains a close personal relationship, and shows concern for the patient’s welfare.
8. **Community-based mental health care facility.** A mental facility outside a mental hospital.

## Chapter 4. Executive Summary — mandate and scope

### 4.1 Legal and institutional context

The Plan reports that Republic Act (RA) No. 11036, the Mental Health Act, was signed into law on **20 June 2018** and took effect on **5 July 2018**. The Act’s Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) were signed by the Secretary of Health, Dr. Francisco T. Duque III, on **22 January 2019**.

The Executive Summary quotes Section 40 of the Act. It describes the Philippine Council for Mental Health (PCMH, referred to in the quotation as “the Council”) as a policy-making, planning, coordinating and advisory body attached to the Department of Health (DOH). Its stated mandate is to oversee implementation of the Act, particularly protection of the rights and freedoms of people with psychiatric, neurologic and psychosocial needs and delivery of rational, unified and integrated mental health services responsive to Filipinos’ needs.

The Executive Summary also quotes the IRR requirement that, within six months after the IRR took effect, the Council develop an implementation strategic plan including a balanced scorecard with indicators. The plan was to encompass a multi-agency and/or multi-sector coordination mechanism supporting integrated participation by regions, provinces, cities and municipalities through regional and local mental health councils or other appropriate bodies.

### 4.2 What the Plan contains

The document describes itself as the Strategic Plan for the PCMH. It sets out the Council’s vision, mission, goals and mental-health outcome objectives. It details outputs, key activities and targets for the first five years of Mental Health Act implementation, **2019–2023**, and identifies responsible agencies or sectors at national, regional and local levels. It also defines indicators and modes of verification for monitoring and evaluation. An accompanying electronic tool was intended to serve as scorecards for the PCMH and specific agencies.

**Key distinction:** this is a time-bounded strategic implementation plan, not an individual-patient diagnostic or treatment guideline and not, by itself, a post-2023 evaluation.

**Source locator:** printed p. 1 / PDF p. 15.

## Chapter 5. Overview of the Situation — historical evidence cited by the Plan

### 5.1 Global and Philippine context

The Plan cites the *World Health Report* (2001) as estimating that one in four people globally would experience mental or neurological disorders at some point in life. It reports estimated global prevalence of depression at **4.4%** and anxiety disorders at **3.6%**. For the Western Pacific, it reports that one fifth of global cases came from the region, with **3.6%** affected by depressive disorders and **2.9%** by anxiety.

For the Philippines, the Plan reports **3.3%**, or **3,298,652 Filipinos**, living with depression, and a suicide mortality rate of **3.2 per 100,000 population**. It cites the 2015 Global School-based Student Health Survey as finding that among students aged 13–17, **16.8%** had attempted suicide and **11.6%** had seriously considered attempting suicide in the preceding 12 months; **7.1%** reported having used drugs at least once in their lives (WHO, 2015).

The Young Adult Fertility and Sexuality Survey figures cited by the Plan report drug use among ages 15–24 in **7.1% of males** and **0.8% of females**, with a mean initiation age of **17.3 years** for both. The proportion of youth who had ever thought about suicide declined from **13.4% in 2002** to **8.7% in 2013**; the proportion who had attempted suicide remained around **3%** in both survey rounds (DRDF and UPPI, 2014).

The Plan states that mental, neuropsychological and substance-use disorders accounted for **2,311,800 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)**—years lost due to illness, disability or premature death—and identifies depressive disorders, anxiety disorders and epilepsy as major contributors to healthy years lost (WHO, 2018).

### 5.4 Financing, workforce and facilities

Under “Out-of-pocket payments,” the Plan cites WHO (2018) and states that the Philippine government spent **0.22% of total health expenditure** on mental health, approximately **₱12.19 per capita**, and that people with mental disorders paid mostly or entirely out of pocket for services and medicines.

Under “Meager human resources for mental health,” it reports **2,051 mental health professionals** in government and non-government settings, including psychiatrists, child psychiatrists, other doctors, nurses, psychologists, social workers, occupational therapists and other paid workers; 60 were child psychiatrists. Counting these professionals and other health workers with some health-care or mental-health-care training, the source reports **2.02 mental health workers per 100,000 Filipinos** (WHO, 2018). Professional association figures cited are **567 psychiatrists** (PPA, 2019), **483 neurologists** (PNA, 2019), and **3,783 registered guidance counsellors** (PGCA, 2019). The Commission on Human Rights was reported to have 147 Special Investigators and 84 lawyers nationally.

Per 100,000 population, the Plan lists **0.518 psychiatrists, 0.885 psychologists, 0.30 occupational therapists and 0.26 speech therapists** working in the mental-health sector. It reports **0.001 mental hospitals, 0.083 mental-health units in general hospitals, 0.211 mental-health outpatient facilities, 0.002 mental-health day-treatment facilities and 0.058 community residential facilities** per 100,000. It also reports **4.129 beds in mental hospitals, 1.324 mental-health beds in general hospitals and 2.045 beds in community residential facilities** per 100,000 (WHO, 2017).

### 5.5 Dominance of hospital-based services

The Plan reports that **82,277 Filipinos** with psychosis, bipolar disorder and depression were treated annually, corresponding to **80.89 per 100,000 population**. It reports **70.8 visits per 100,000** to mental-health outpatient facilities attached to hospitals; visits to community-based and other outpatient facilities, and to outpatient services specifically for children and adolescents, were not reported. There were **3,365 inpatients**, while the number of involuntary admissions was unknown.

Per 100,000 population, the Plan reports:

- Mental-hospital beds: **4.13**, with **11.42 annual admissions** to these hospitals.
- General-hospital psychiatric-unit beds: **1.32**, with **3.66 annual admissions**.
- Child and adolescent inpatient beds: **0.10**, with **0.28 admissions**.
- Residential-care beds: **2.04**; annual admissions were not reported.
- Forensic inpatient-unit beds: none known.

The Plan describes hospital stays as usually long-term: **40%** of mental-hospital inpatients stayed less than one year, **35%** stayed one to five years, and **25%** stayed more than five years. It also reports that more than **75%** of discharged inpatients received a follow-up outpatient visit within one month (WHO, 2018).

**Learning explanation (not a separate source finding):** the source’s combination of workforce/facility scarcity, out-of-pocket costs, unreported community visits, and long inpatient stays supports why the later strategic framework pays attention to service access, community-level delivery, financing, information and governance. This is a synthesis of the listed source themes, not an independent assessment of present-day services.

## Chapter 6. Policies on Mental Health

This section situates the Plan among international rights and development commitments, global and regional WHO plans, and Philippine laws and administrative issuances. The section is a description of how the source frames the policy environment, not an independent legal analysis.

### 6.1 International rights and development instruments

- **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD):** the Plan describes it as protecting persons with disabilities from discrimination based on disability, including people with long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments. The extracted passage ends mid-sentence on PDF p. 23; the complete instrument and any further detail should be checked in the page image if needed.
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights:** the Plan highlights the right to a standard of living adequate for health and well-being, including food, clothing, housing, medical care and necessary social services, and security in the event of sickness and disability (the Plan cites “UNHRD, 1948”).
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs):** the Plan frames the SDGs as a universal call to end poverty, protect the planet, and support peace and prosperity. It highlights SDG 3, “ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages,” and three targets: **3.4**, reducing premature mortality from non-communicable diseases by one third through prevention and treatment while promoting mental health and well-being; **3.5**, strengthening prevention and treatment of substance abuse, including narcotic drug abuse and harmful use of alcohol; and **3.8**, universal health coverage, financial-risk protection, access to quality essential health services and affordable essential medicines and vaccines. The Plan also notes that multiple SDGs address social determinants of mental health.

### 6.2 WHO global and Western Pacific plans

The Plan states that the Sixty-fifth World Health Assembly adopted resolution **WHA65.4** on the global burden of mental disorders in May 2012. WHO then developed the **Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2020** to guide national action plans. Its overall goal is described as promoting mental well-being, preventing mental disorders, providing care, enhancing recovery, promoting human rights, and reducing mortality, morbidity and disability among people with mental disorders.

The source lists four objectives in its extracted text: (1) strengthen effective leadership and governance for mental health; (2) provide comprehensive, integrated and responsive mental-health and social-care services in community settings; (3) implement mental-health promotion and prevention strategies; and (4) strengthen information systems, evidence and research. Although the paragraph says “six objectives,” only four are enumerated in the source passage. This discrepancy is retained as a source-text issue rather than silently supplying additional objectives.

The **Regional Agenda for Implementing the Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2020 in the Western Pacific** is described as providing phased options—core, expanded and comprehensive actions—to reflect different needs and resources. The Plan emphasizes its all-of-society and whole-of-government approach (WHO, 2015).

### 6.3 Philippine laws and issuances named in the section

- **RA No. 11036 (2018), Mental Health Act.** The Plan says it establishes a national mental-health policy, affirms the basic right to mental health and protects fundamental rights of people requiring mental-health services. The source lists provisions concerning service-user and stakeholder rights; quality of public mental-health services; mental-health education and promotion in educational institutions and workplaces; research and development; government agency duties and responsibilities; establishment of the PCMH; and prohibited acts and penalties. The Plan identifies Annex 1 as the Act and Annex 2 as its IRR.
- **RA No. 11223 (2018), Universal Health Care Act.** Described as reforming the health-care system through a systemic approach to financing, service delivery, local health systems, regulation, governance and accountability. The Plan notes requirements for population-based health services with a primary-care provider network, epidemiologic surveillance systems and health-promotion programs.
- **RA No. 10754 (2016), Act Expanding the Benefits and Privileges of Persons with Disability.** The Plan says this amended the Magna Carta for Persons with Disabilities and expanded benefits, including at least a 20% discount and VAT exemption on the purchase of certain goods and services.
- **RA No. 7277 (1992), Magna Carta for Disabled Persons.** Described as supporting rehabilitation, self-development and self-reliance; improving overall well-being by developing skills and potentials and integrating persons with disabilities into society; identifying covered persons (including people with mental illness), rights and privileges, and prohibited discriminatory acts; and mandating special-education centers in all regions for people with visual, hearing and intellectual disabilities.
- **DOH Administrative Order No. 2016-0039 (2016), Revised Operational Framework for a Comprehensive National Mental Health Program.** The Plan describes guiding principles and an action framework for a comprehensive program covering promotion, prevention, treatment and rehabilitation for people with mental, neurological and substance-abuse (MNS) disorders. It assigns roles to sectors for promoting well-being, preventing MNS disorders and other addictions, providing care, enhancing recovery, and reducing morbidity, disability and mortality.
- **DOH Administrative Order No. 2007-0009 (2007), Operational Framework for the Sustainable Establishment of a Mental Health Program.** Described as earlier guidance for government and private-sector stakeholders to develop and implement the national mental-health policy through plans and programs. It addresses service delivery, financing, regulation and governance, and strategies for everyday well-being, extreme life experiences, substance abuse and other addictions, and mental disorders. The source notes national and regional implementation committees.

## Chapter 7. The Gaps in Fostering Mental Health and Wellbeing

### 7.1 Overall framing

The section cites a technical report by Lucita S. Lazo dated 8 August 2019, *Theory of Change of the Philippine Mental Health Strategic Plan 2019–2023*, submitted to the WHO Philippines Country Office. It states that although the Philippines seeks “Health for All,” mental health care had historically received low priority. A PCHRD-sponsored study on the national mental-health research agenda and gaps implied by the 2018 Mental Health Act are cited as background.

The section groups the gaps into four broad needs: (1) proactive promotion of mental health and prevention of mental disorders; (2) adequate mental-health information; (3) stronger leadership and governance; and (4) sufficient mental-health services. It also refers to the population of 110 million in the source’s historical context. This population figure should not be read as a current population estimate.

### 7.2 A. Need for a human-rights-based social environment for promotion

The section quotes Section 2 of the Mental Health Act, which declares a national policy to promote people’s well-being by ensuring mental health is valued, promoted and protected. It argues that the service system should maintain health and wellness rather than focus only on treating illness. Promotion therefore concerns the majority described in the Plan as well or asymptomatic, including people and groups with specific cultural backgrounds who sustain healthy, satisfactory lives and contribute to their communities.

The source identifies socio-economic and environmental stressors—especially poverty, modernization, technology, violence, climate change, disasters and extreme life experiences—as important pressures. It calls for resilience-building and reduction of vulnerability as part of promotion.

Stigma is described as constraining help-seeking. The Plan calls for mental-health literacy that can translate into help-seeking behavior, including public education for parents, teachers, students, service providers, guidance counsellors and barangay health workers. It also calls for Philippine-context research on stigma, noting that existing definitions are based on Western studies. The text observes that daily basic needs may be prioritized over mental-health treatment and calls for research on improving help-seeking in schools and workplaces.

The section argues that diagnosis/treatment interventions patterned on Western models have overshadowed indigenous and culturally sensitive approaches, and that a disease- or clinical orientation has focused on people already ill rather than the larger well/asymptomatic population.

**Community mental-health service delivery needs listed in the section:**
- mechanisms for community reintegration of survivors;
- more mental-health professionals and workers at grassroots level;
- accessible services integrated into health systems down to barangay level;
- sustainable community-based service-delivery systems;
- protocols for handling mental-health cases at barangay level;
- programs and capacity building for health workers, including long-term capacity building in post-disaster communities; and
- assessment of implementation of the WHO mhGAP program.

Public education is expected by the source to reduce stigma. It calls for communication strategies that make evidence-based research available to the public; accessible information through school curricula and barangay health education; and education that builds capacity to cope with psychosocial stressors of modern life. Parents, teachers, barangay health workers, youth and other socialization agents are identified as people whose capacity should be developed. Referral networks and information on how to access services should be widely disseminated to facilitate help-seeking.

The section recommends promoting mental health and well-being in families, communities, schools and workplaces across the life course, from birth to maturity. It connects promotion/prevention to optimizing brain health and reducing stressors at each life stage at national and local levels.

### 7.3 B. Inadequate mental-health information

The Plan says Section 29 of the Mental Health Law IRR calls for research to produce information for a culturally relevant national mental-health program that incorporates indigenous concepts and practices. It states that a national epidemiologic study should be conducted at regular intervals determined by the PCMH.

The section calls for a mental-health database that can incorporate research produced each year by graduates of psychiatry, neurology and psychology training centers and colleges. It cites the NCMH Strategic Needs Assessment (July 2016) as calling for a unified information system and standardized mental-health service delivery system, requiring collaboration across a fragmented public/private provider network.

It cites the WHO Assessment Instrument for Mental Health Systems (WHO-AIMS) report’s recommendation for community surveillance of specific mental disorders, including epidemiologic studies of prevalence and risk factors by disorder type, region and nationwide. The studies should estimate not only prevalence but also magnitude to guide policy, planning, monitoring and evaluation.

The Plan says disease-burden estimation was significantly hampered by a lack of national epidemiologic data. It notes that UP-PGH was conducting an epidemiologic survey, with results expected in 2020 at the time of writing. It says the literature provided information on suicide, suicidal ideation, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and depression; few studies addressed PTSD and substance-use risk factors; and research on schizophrenia was described as nil. It quotes Bautista et al. (2018) as noting no study to date on schizophrenia outcomes or factors affecting prognosis. These are claims reported in the historical Plan and should not be interpreted as a current inventory of research.

The section states that vital information is needed to rationalize the mental-health delivery system and determine corresponding resources. It recommends a life-course approach so needs at all stages of life are deliberately considered.

### 7.4 C. Weak leadership and governance

Governance is described as encompassing policy-making, program planning, resource allocation, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, involving multiple stakeholders. Its end results are the mental-health services delivered to constituents in a jurisdiction. Mental-health governance assigns duties, roles and responsibilities to duty bearers. The Act’s Chapter VII is described as defining the governance architecture, with the PCMH attached to DOH identified as the highest policy-making body on mental health.

The section notes that mental-health service delivery is assigned to LGUs under the Philippines’ decentralized governance system, but characterizes results in prior years as inadequate, inaccessible and ineffective services. It cites Mental Health Act Section 38 on LGU duties and Section 39 on establishing or upgrading hospitals and facilities with qualified personnel, equipment and supplies to provide services and address psychiatric emergencies.

It cites Section 37 as assigning DSWD responsibilities to refer service users to appropriate facilities and providers; provide or facilitate access to public/group housing, counselling, therapy, livelihood training and skills development; formulate and implement community resilience and psychosocial well-being training; provide psychosocial support during and after disasters/calamities; and develop training and capacity-building programs.

The section calls for clearer national/local government roles and more active engagement of local officials in community-based services. Drawing on the Lancet Commission’s “mental health is everybody’s business” framing, it argues for participation by NGOs and stronger organization of families and people with lived experience. It calls for a paradigm shift among duty bearers and orientation of the PCMH to the shifts articulated in the Lancet report.

Specific governance gaps include:
- absence of local-government policies to implement national mental-health policy;
- inadequate participation by non-health sectors and people with lived experience;
- insufficient systematic identification of barriers to help-seeking and service provision;
- capacity gaps in developing and implementing programs and establishing community-based services;
- inadequate resources and insufficient integration of mental-health services into primary care;
- need for prevalence data analyzed by geographic and political district to clarify duty-bearer accountability;
- need to attend to care quality and build capacity among professionals and communities;
- lack of data on public and private facilities and on the mental-health status of professionals named in the source (psychiatrists, neurologists and psychologists);
- inadequate grassroots human resources and the need to consider permanent government positions (plantilla items);
- need to map programs/services and assess effectiveness, accessibility, responsiveness and other implementation factors, including comparison of facility-based and community-based programs; and
- need for operations research supporting DOH/PhilHealth accreditation of mental-health-friendly facilities and examination of financial sustainability, costs and possible expansion of PhilHealth outpatient coverage including medication or other mental-health coverage.

### 7.5 D. Insufficient mental-health services

The section quotes Section 4 of the Mental Health Act defining mental-health services as psychosocial, psychiatric or neurologic activities/programs across promotion, prevention, treatment and aftercare, provided by mental-health facilities and professionals.

It lists provisions in Sections 15–23 of the Act: quality of services; services at community level; community-based mental-health care; reporting requirements; psychiatric, psychosocial and neurologic services in regional, provincial and tertiary hospitals; duties/responsibilities of facilities; drug-screening services; suicide prevention; and public awareness.

Service gaps are framed from both **claim holders** (families and clients) and **duty bearers** (national/local government agencies in health and non-health sectors). On the demand side, the Plan says groups should be differentiated: it estimates that about **20%** of the population would need specialist care due to mental-health conditions, while the larger **80%** without diagnosed mental-health symptoms would need maintenance of well-being. This is a historical estimate stated in the source, not a current prevalence figure.

Stakeholder consultations are described as highlighting a need for a suicide-prevention program in response to a reported increasing trend among youth, and an effective substance-abuse program addressing addiction to drugs, tobacco and alcohol. The section connects these concerns to Act Section 21 (Drug Screening Services) and Section 22 (Suicide Prevention).

Accessibility is a central concern. Users and potential users need information about which providers offer which services, how to reach them, and the procedures/protocols for access. The section calls for an institutional referral system and protocols for managing mental-health problems across settings and levels—from barangay, school and workplace to self-care and expert care. It notes that patients and families may struggle to identify referral networks because service databases are lacking or unavailable; counsellors identified this as a critical issue for cases needing urgent pharmacological intervention.

On the supply side, the Plan highlights availability, quality, affordability and sustainability of services and of psychotropic medicines and antiepileptic drugs. It calls for psychosocial support during and after natural disasters, calamities and other extreme life experiences. It also notes limited mental-health human resources and the need to protect the available professionals and carers from burnout.

## Chapter 8. The Road to the Strategic Plan 2019–2023

### 8.1 Mandate and timeline

The section repeats the key legal timeline: RA No. 11036 was signed on **20 June 2018** and took effect on **5 July 2018**. Section 40 established the PCMH as a DOH-attached policy-making, planning, coordinating and advisory body tasked with overseeing the Act’s implementation, rights protection, and rational, unified, integrated services responsive to Filipinos’ needs.

The IRR was signed by the Secretary of Health on **22 January 2019**. As quoted by the Plan, it required the Council to develop an implementation strategic plan including a balanced scorecard with indicators and a multi-agency/multi-sector mechanism for participation by regions, provinces, cities and municipalities through local/regional councils or other suitable bodies.

### 8.2 Technical working group

The DOH, in coordination with WHO, convened stakeholders—especially those involved in developing the Act and IRR—to propose terms of reference and nominate members of a technical working group (TWG) for the PCMH plan. The DOH then issued a Department Personnel Order creating the TWG. It was chaired by the Director of the DOH Disease Prevention and Control Bureau (DPCB), with the Chief of the Essential Non-Communicable Diseases Division (ENCDD) as vice-chair.

The TWG included government/duty bearers, persons with lived experience (PLE)/service users, service providers and academe. National government agencies listed were:

- Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)
- Commission on Human Rights (CHR)
- Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD)
- Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE)
- Civil Service Commission (CSC)
- Department of Education (DepEd)
- Commission on Higher Education (CHED)
- Technical Education and Skills Development Authority (TESDA)
- National Bureau of Investigation (NBI)
- Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth)
- National Center for Mental Health (NCMH)

People with lived experience were represented by Youth for Mental Health Coalition, Inc. (YMHC), #MentalHealthPH (MHPH), and the Alliance of Filipino Families for Mental Health (AFFMH).

Service providers and academic organizations named were the Association of Municipal Health Officers of the Philippines (AMHOP), Philippine General Hospital–Department of Neuroscience (PGH), World Association for Psychosocial Rehabilitation (WAPR), Psychological Association of the Philippines (PAP), Philippine Mental Health Association (PMHA), Philippine Psychiatric Association (PPA), and Philippine Guidance Counselling Association (PGCA).

### 8.3 Consultation and drafting process

The Plan says additional stakeholders were consulted to identify priority issues, actions and areas of convergence. Four consultations were held with: (1) DOH; (2) other government agencies and duty bearers; (3) persons with lived experience; and (4) service providers and academics. Consultation results and a review of relevant documents were brought to the TWG. The group developed the Plan through **three workshops, each lasting three days**, and presented the resulting Strategic Plan 2019–2023 to the PCMH for approval.

## Cross-chapter synthesis (learning explanation, not source quotation)

The opening sections establish a logic for the rest of the Plan:

1. The Act and IRR supply the institutional mandate and call for a time-bounded plan with indicators and coordination.
2. The situation overview presents historical burden, suicide, financing, workforce, facility and service-use figures, including important reporting gaps.
3. The policy section links national planning to rights instruments, SDG targets, WHO plans and Philippine laws/administrative issuances.
4. The gaps section argues for a broader focus than treatment alone: promotion and prevention, culturally relevant information, multi-sector governance, accessible community services, sustainable financing, and workforce support.
5. The development section documents consultation across government, lived-experience groups, providers and academia before the TWG submitted the Plan to PCMH.

This synthesis is a study aid. It does not claim that the historical gaps persist unchanged today or that the Plan’s later activities achieved their intended outcomes.
